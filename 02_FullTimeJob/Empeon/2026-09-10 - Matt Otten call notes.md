## What these actually mean — the argument is in here

**1. Government reimbursement tied to patient satisfaction and readmission
rates is the forcing function.** Empeon's buyers are not choosing scheduling
software on preference. Their revenue is regulated against KPIs that staffing
directly drives — understaffing hurts satisfaction scores and readmissions, and
those hit reimbursement. That converts an HR purchase into a **finance**
purchase.

For marketing this is the whole opening: content that ranks for readmission,
patient-satisfaction and reimbursement queries is **buying-stage** content, not
top-of-funnel. It is the same insight as the Align work — the winning queries
were `paychex vs paylocity` and `ukg pro implementation partner`, vendor-selection
terms, not brand defence.

**2. "No contracts, MRR, 98.5% renewal" is the case for marketing spend.**
No contracts means retention is earned monthly. A 98.5% renewal rate means the
product is not the problem. **So growth is almost entirely an acquisition
problem** — which is precisely the gap the Semrush read shows: 93% of traffic on
one channel, zero paid keywords, 2.7% AI Overview presence.

Say it plainly to Nack: you keep 98.5% of what you win, so every dollar of
growth turns on how many you win, and right now one channel carries all of it.

**3. "65% of customers — raise my hand white paper" is the most important line
in the notes.** If roughly two-thirds of customers came through a gated
white-paper hand-raise, that is a **proven owned-channel acquisition motion**,
already validated with their own money. It is not a hypothesis to sell them.

Which means the pitch is not "let me try content marketing." It is: *your
best-performing acquisition motion is one you already run, it is entirely
owned-channel, and nobody has instrumented it end to end.* That is exactly the
work sample — 22 qualified leads graded by evidence tier, and the 41% follow-up
gap it surfaced.

**4. "Pb&J reporting" and "shift swap" are product surface.** Shift swap is a
scheduling feature for acute care. Both are worth knowing as vocabulary; neither
is the marketing argument.
